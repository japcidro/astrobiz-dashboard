# Video breakdown: "I Studied Every $100M Static Ad So You Don't Have To"

Source: adswithsimon (Simon, of the agency Static), YouTube `ZsyROsPLnDE`, 16:59, published 2026-11-06. Studied from the transcript and one frame every five seconds. Timestamps are approximate.

## 0:00 The mistake

- On screen: an AG1 gummies ad ("Original ads") next to a near-identical "CG1" apple-gummies knock-off ("Copied ads"). Later (4:50) the same pair plus a Hexclad knife ad next to a copied mascara ad, titled "Similar ads".
- Point: people open the Ad Library, find an ad they like and recreate it. They copy what it looks like without knowing why the brand made it.
- Credentials shown: brand logo wall (AG1, Jones Road, Hexclad, Fussy, Popsockets, Surreal, Hollow, Oar, Maëlys…), "$50,000,000 in ad spend".

## 1:05 Three brands, three approaches

Title cards under each ad: "AG1: offer", "RIDGE: replacement", "JONES ROAD: credibility".

### AG1: selling the offer (1:15)

- The ad: cream background, serif headline "Free Welcome Kit + Omega 3 + Vitamin D3+K2!", a neon-green highlighted strip "AG1'S IMMUNE SUPPORT BUNDLE", then a grid of six items: the pouch ("30 Day Supply of AG1"), shaker, canister, travel packs, dropper bottle, capsule bottle, each with a crossed-out price and "Free" ($14, $14, $15, $29, $39). Fine print at the bottom.
- Why: a premium supplement = high price, slow results, a subscription. Hesitation is high, so the ad gives an extra reason to try: $111 in free extras, crossed-out prices make the value obvious, five freebies make the first order feel safe, "limited time" adds urgency. The kit also builds a daily ritual that keeps people subscribed (longer LTV).
- If you cannot offer freebies: lead with the benefit and back it with proof (clinical claims).

### Ridge: selling the replacement (2:05)

- The ad: dark studio background with a spotlight, a purple metal wallet in the centre, headline in white condensed caps "NOT LEATHER. NOT BORING. NOT BULKY.", subhead "Aerospace materials. Zero bulk. One wallet for the rest of your life", four pill labels pointing at the wallet (RFID Blocking, Lifetime Durability, Holds 1-12 Cards, Modular), a black CTA strip "CHECK OUT AT RIDGE.COM". He underlines the headline, then the subhead.
- Why: the visual never shows a traditional wallet, but the headline compares against it. The viewer pictures their own wallet and sees the problem. "You don't need a new wallet until your current one feels outdated." A cutaway shows a brown leather bifold "vs" the metal wallet.
- Contrast beats a feature list: features (RFID, 1–12 cards) only matter once someone already wants to switch. Comparing against something familiar makes the benefit instant.
- "Not boring" is an identity hook: it turns the old wallet into a statement about its owner.

### Jones Road: selling through credibility (3:05)

- The ad: top two-thirds a black-and-white portrait of Bobbi Brown on light grey, serif headline at left "Classic Favorites, Reimagined", a handwritten "xx, Bobbi" signature; bottom third a white strip with "Five essentials, chosen by legendary makeup artist, Bobbi Brown", "The Bobbi Kit 6.0", and the five products in colour. He highlights "Five essentials, chosen by".
- Why: borrowed trust (decades as a makeup artist = if she picked it, it works), curation (a bundle becomes an expert's recommendation and removes decision fatigue), personal touch (portrait + signature = a note from her), premium perception (her name justifies the price, the kit feels like an insider pack).
- Without a known founder: borrow credibility elsewhere (makeup artists, dermatologists, creators). "Who's the authority figure for your brand or product?"

### Zoom out (4:00)

- AG1 sells the offer because a premium subscription needs a reason to start. Ridge sells the switch because everyone already owns a wallet. Jones Road sells trust because beauty buyers want an expert's pick.
- Smaller brands can use: the Ridge angle (call out the old product) and the authority idea. Big-brand advantages they cannot use: the famous founder, editorial shoots, $100 in free items.

## 5:05 Borrow / don't copy / surprisingly simple

Title cards: "An ad I would borrow from" (Jones Road), "An ad I wouldn't copy" (AG1 offer), and the AG1 meme.

### Borrow: the authority figure (5:15)

- Works for every brand. The figure is not always the same person. Doctor ads are big and they work, but the right doctor depends on the target: on screen a young surgeon in scrubs and mask labelled "Gen Z doctor" beside a grey-haired doctor in a white coat labelled "Gen X & millennial doctor".
- Not only doctors: a grandma, a psychologist, an athlete, a muscular guy. Each is an authority to a different audience. One brand can have more than one.
- Later (8:05) four versions of the same editorial layout: Jones Road/Bobbi; "Grandma's Remedy: Natural wisdom, for generations… chosen by our beloved matriarch, Grandma Nora · The Gentle Care Kit"; "Dr. Joseph's Practice: Understanding, for life's challenges… guided by experienced psychiatrist Dr. Joseph"; "Antio's Athletics: Built on sweat, proven by time… chosen by champion and veteran athlete, Antio · The Athlete's Recovery Kit". Same structure, only the figure changes.

### Borrow: the sorting line (5:50)

- Ridge chain ad: a bearded man in a cowboy hat and sunglasses on the right, a large silver curb chain in the lower-left foreground, headline top-left in bold caps "FOR GUYS WHO DON'T SWITCH JEWELRY.", small caps underneath "RIDGE 5MM CURB CHAIN", logo bottom-right.
- It calls out a type of person: the guy who wears one chain every day. If that is you, you stop. If not, the ad was not for you anyway.
- Side by side with the wallet ad, labelled "Person you are" vs "Product". The product headline is about the product; this one is about the person, "probably a stronger angle to start with".
- Any brand can do it and test several, one per audience. On screen, three made-up examples in the same layout: "FOR THOSE WHO NEVER WAIT." (perfume, older man), "FOR GIRLS WHO DON'T DO SUBTLE." (red lipstick, young woman), "FOR GUYS WHO DO ORDINARY." (ring, young man).

### Don't copy (6:30)

- Jones Road's editorial format (flagged with a warning icon "Editorial format"): it only works with a famous founder (Bobbi Brown, Hailey Bieber for Rhode). Without the name it is just a nice black-and-white photo.
- AG1's offer: it needs a product ecosystem (shaker, canister, travel packs, Omega 3, D3, Q10 in one bundle). Most small brands have one or two products. On screen: a generic "Vibrant Green 1" pouch with the bullets "Lead with the product & its benefits" and "Use a simple offer: free shipping, one add-on, money-back guarantee".

### Surprisingly simple: the AG1 meme (7:30)

- White card. Top: a crying line-drawn face with the text 'Your "daily supplement routine" is just 1 glass of AG1 Pro with 80+ science-backed nutrients like creatine, zinc carnosine, vitamins, minerals, and adaptogens'. Bottom: a bearded, sunglasses-wearing face with "Yes." and "(got a free Yeti Shaker too**)". Fine print "Offer valid for new subscribers only."
- AG1 runs polished editorial ads and then this. It speaks to millennial and Gen Z men in their language. Low production, same message as the other ads (one glass replaces a pill stack, 80+ nutrients, free shaker). The format makes the message feel native to the feed. "The message matters much more than the polish."

## 8:15 The agency's own winner: a native comparison

- The ad: a phone photo of two economy plane seats by a window. The left seat has a black grid seat cushion; over it, in green handwriting with a tick: "10 hrs to dublin, tailbone completely fine". The right seat is bare; over it in white handwriting with a cross: "absolutely not". No logo, no button, no headline.
- "$15,000 in the last 30 days". Warning-icon list: "No famous founder · No big budget · No product ecosystem". You could swap the brand and it would still work.
- It is clear about who it is for (one specific person, like the Ridge chain line) and it lands in one second. "If it needs a polished production to make sense, the idea isn't strong enough."

### Why they tested it and what happened (11:00)

- On screen: "Specific situation everyone recognizes · Before vs after format · Comparison format, looks like a real person's post".
- Performance: "Top performer in the last 30 days & it's a TOF static ad · $15K spent, 144 purchases, $101.80 CPA, 1.79 ROAS · Frequency 1.63: it's still fresh and can keep scaling".
- The polished version of the same angle: a smiling model in white loungewear on a folding chair with the cushion, navy background, headline "YOUR PLANE SEAT HAS LESS PADDING THAN THIS CHAIR.", "SO WHY LET EVERY FLIGHT DESTROY YOUR TAILBONE?", a blue button "Try it risk-free for 60 days.", "COMFORT. EVERYWHERE.", brand name. Low spend; Meta did not push it.
- On screen: "The angle was right in both of the ads · The result depended on how native the execution was". The handwritten one looks like a person's post, the polished one looks like an ad.
- "A great ROAS on $200 doesn't tell you much." An ad that spends $15,000 at an acceptable CPA has proven it can scale.
- Next: variations of the winning angle. On screen three versions of the same photo idea: an office chair ("8 hrs at the office, tailbone completely fine ✓ / absolutely not ✗"), the plane, a coach seat. Also named: a long drive, a stadium seat. "If an angle doesn't get spend after several executions, move on." Plan for the client: "More native, low polish statics".

## 9:15 Reading the Ad Library

- You cannot see spend or profit, but you can learn from what stays live and what gets versions.
- Look for the oldest ads relative to the account. AG1 rotates fast; an arrow labelled "Oldest ad" points at a gummies ad "Started running on 21 Sep 2026", about five weeks. The ad itself: a hand holding gummies over a pouch, a small text box "Tastes like apple and strawberry. Works like part of my routine."
- Old does not mean profitable. Warning list: "Small budget · Used for re-targeting · Not turned off yet". "It tells you where to look, not what's working."
- Multiple versions of one concept: the "Instagram story ad format". A real-looking top-down photo of the products on a counter, white story text boxes ("Okay, this immune support bundle is insane 😉", "free Vitamin D3+K2 & Omega3 (limited time only though!)"), a second version on a wooden table ("not to influence you but… AG1's October offer is kindaaa 👀", "free welcome kit + omega3 + vitamin d3+k2??", "SOLD! 😊"), a creator handle. What stays: story-style photo, text boxes, a handle. What changes: creator, product, message. That is what doubling down looks like from outside.
- What he would test next for them: the meme message inside the story format ("One glass instead of 10 pills?" / "Yes.").

## 12:45 Building a new ad from two borrowed ideas

- Formula on screen: Jones Road ad (tag "Authority figure") + Ridge chain ad (tag "Sorting line") = the new ad. "People will trust whoever they look up to" and they stop when a headline describes them.
- Consumer psychology card: "Iconic pop stars · TikTok · Gen Z women (20–25) · Not being thin, but being strong". Gym footage of a young woman drinking from a shaker.
- Product: "LAZY GIRL. Creatine Gummies", a frosted jar with a blush-pink minimal label, gummies all one colour. The name owns the insult in the headline; the jar looks like something she would leave on her counter, not a supplement bottle.
- Kept from the originals: "Authority figure audience relates to · Headline aims at one specific person". Changed: "Archetype the audience is into right now i.e. pop stars" instead of a founder; made with AI; tone funny, not editorial.
- The image: a fit blonde woman of about fifty, a pop-star archetype, standing backstage and holding the jar up toward the camera at chest height. Arrows label the cues: "Headset mic", "Sequined jacket"; behind her a dressing room with mirror lights, a clothes rack with sequins, flight cases. "Without those cues, she's just a blonde woman at the gym."
- The copy, on a plain band at the top: a thin condensed serif line "Your ex said gummies were lazy." and under it, bigger, bold pink caps "GOOD THING YOU DON'T BELIEVE IN GHOSTS."
- Why it works: it speaks to something these women care about today, not letting anyone make them feel small. The ex called her lazy for choosing gummies over powder; he is a ghost now. She listens instead to a woman she trusts: older, strong, still on stage, living proof of what she wants to become.
- Why a pop star and not a doctor or athlete: "This audience doesn't need to be convinced that creatine works · They just need to feel it's for them."
- Test plan on screen: the pop-star version and a second authority, a "boyfriend athlete" archetype (young man in a locker room with a tennis racket holding the jar, headline "Your ex said gummies were lazy. / THAT'S WHY HE'S YOUR EX."), each with and without the headline = four versions. It shows whether the face alone does the work.
- If it performs: keep the winning archetype and test new sorting lines aimed at different villains (the jealous best friend, the strict mother).

## 16:30 Close

Learn from their approach, do not recreate their ads. Start with why they chose that message and whether the idea could work for your own customer.
